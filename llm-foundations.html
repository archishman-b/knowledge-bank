Chapter 1

Introduction: What Is Behind the Text Box?

Setting out the questions this text will answer

When you navigate to a service like ChatGPT and type a message into that familiar text box, you are interacting with one of the most complex artifacts ever constructed by human engineering. And yet, for most users, the experience is deceptively simple: you write something, you press Enter, and words come back.

"What is behind this text box? What should we be putting in there, and what are these words generated back? How does this work — and what, exactly, are you talking to?"

— Andrej Karpathy

This question motivates everything that follows. The goal is to give you robust mental models for thinking about large language models: what they are capable of, where they reliably fail, and what sharp edges to be aware of in practice.

Chapter 2

Pre-Training: Ingesting the Internet

How raw web text becomes a training dataset

The first step in pre-training is assembling a training dataset from internet text. The goal is a collection that is simultaneously large, high-quality, and diverse. Diversity matters because the model's eventual breadth of knowledge is bounded by the breadth of the data it sees.

Hugging Face's FineWeb is a publicly documented example, illustrating the multi-stage curation process that all major labs replicate internally.

The Curation Pipeline

  1. Web Crawl — A crawler downloads raw HTML from billions of web pages.
  2. HTML Processing — Boilerplate, navigation menus, and markup are stripped.
  3. Quality Filtering — Heuristics remove spam, duplicates, and low-information pages.
  4. Language Filtering — Pages are classified by language; FineWeb retains pages more than 65% English.
  5. Deduplication — Near-duplicate documents are removed to prevent over-fitting.
Scale in Perspective FineWeb's final dataset occupies roughly 44 terabytes on disk — small enough to fit on a single modern hard drive, yet containing an enormous breadth of human knowledge. Aggressive filtering reduces the vast raw internet to a manageable, curated slice.

The critical insight: not all data is equal. Quality filtering is as important as quantity in determining the final model's capabilities.

Design Choice The choice of which languages to include directly determines multilingual performance. A model trained mostly on English will underperform in Spanish, Mandarin, or Hindi.
Chapter 3

Tokenization: From Text to Numbers

How language is encoded into a form neural networks can process

Neural networks operate on numbers, not characters or words. Tokenization is the process of converting raw text into numerical representations.

A tokenizerAn algorithm that converts text into a sequence of integer IDs, each representing a sub-word chunk. Most modern tokenizers use Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). breaks text into chunks called tokens. Common words become single tokens; rare words are split across multiple tokens. Each unique token is mapped to an integer ID.

Definition — Token A token is the atomic unit of text an LLM processes — typically a sub-word chunk, not a full word, not a single character. GPT-4's tokenizer contains approximately 100,000 distinct tokens.

Why Tokenization Causes Surprising Failures

Because the model processes tokens rather than characters, tasks requiring character-level manipulation — spelling, counting letters, reversing words — are disproportionately difficult. The model must decompose a token back into characters, a step for which it receives little training signal.

Practical Implication For letter counting, spell-checking, or string reversal, instruct the model to use a code interpreter rather than rely on its own processing. The Python interpreter handles character-level operations exactly; the LLM does not.
Key Terms
Token — A sub-word text chunk mapped to an integer ID.
Vocabulary — The full set of tokens a tokenizer recognizes (~100K for GPT-4).
BPE — Byte-Pair Encoding; the most common tokenization algorithm.

Chapter 4

Neural Networks and Training

Weights, loss functions, and gradient descent

Once the training data is tokenized, a neural networkA mathematical function composed of many layers of operations parameterized by billions of learned numbers called weights or parameters. is trained to predict, for any sequence of tokens, what token comes next.

The architecture used in modern LLMs is the Transformer, introduced in 2017. Its key property: every token in the input can directly relate to every other token via attention.

The Training Loop

  1. Sample a window of tokens from the training data.
  2. Pass tokens through the network; produce a probability distribution over what comes next.
  3. Compare prediction to the actual next token; compute the loss — how wrong the prediction was.
  4. Use backpropagationAn algorithm computing, for each parameter, how much a small change in that parameter changes the loss — giving the gradient, the direction of steepest descent. to see how each weight contributed to the error.
  5. Nudge all weights in the direction that reduces loss (gradient descent). Repeat trillions of times.
Key Insight The model is never explicitly taught any fact. It learns everything — grammar, arithmetic, geography, code, reasoning — implicitly, by being forced to predict what text comes next across an enormous diversity of documents.

Scale

Modern LLMs have between 7 billion and 1+ trillion parameters. Training the largest models costs tens to hundreds of millions of dollars in compute — which is why only a handful of organizations can train frontier models from scratch.

The Loss Number During training, a gradually decreasing "loss" indicates the model is learning. A loss near zero would mean perfect prediction — impossible on natural text, as language is inherently uncertain.
Hardware An H100 GPU costs ~$30,000. Training a frontier model requires thousands of them running for months — total compute cost can exceed $100 million.
Chapter 5

The Base Model

What pre-training produces — and what it does not

After pre-training, the result is a base model — a powerful statistical predictor of what text comes next.

Feed it the opening of a Wikipedia article, and it continues a plausible Wikipedia article. Feed it a Python function's start, and it completes plausible Python code. Feed it a dialogue, and it continues the dialogue — potentially speaking as multiple characters simultaneously.

Analogy Karpathy describes a base model as a "document sampler" — it samples text statistically plausible given the internet it trained on. It has no persistent identity, no goals. It is an extraordinarily fluent simulation of text.

What the Model "Knows"

The base model has internalized an enormous amount of world knowledge — but in a probabilistic, lossy way. Information appearing frequently in training data is better represented than rare information. The model is, in a sense, a very high-resolution statistical compression of the internet.

Critical Limitation A base model is not a knowledge base. It cannot look things up; it can only recall (imperfectly) what it saw during training. A base model is also not yet an assistant — it will not answer questions helpfully. It continues whatever pattern you provide.
Where to Try Hyperbolic (hyperbolic.xyz) serves base models including Llama 3.1 Base — a revealing experience very different from interacting with a chat assistant.
Chapter 6

Supervised Fine-Tuning: Teaching Helpfulness

How a document predictor becomes a conversational assistant

The base model knows a great deal but does not behave like an assistant. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) addresses this by training the model on human-written example conversations.

The Data Labeling Process

Companies hire data labelers — often domain experts — who are given detailed labeling instructions specifying ideal assistant behavior: tone, format, refusal policies, uncertainty acknowledgment. Their task: write ideal responses to a diverse collection of prompts. After collecting perhaps one million such pairs, this dataset is used to continue training the base model.

The Core Mental Model When you receive a response from ChatGPT, you are receiving a neural network simulation of what a human data labeler, following OpenAI's instruction manual, would have written. Not a retrieval from a database. Not a lookup. A statistical imitation of human expert writing behavior.

Before and After SFT

Base ModelAfter SFT
Continues documents in any styleResponds in structured, helpful format
No consistent identity or valuesConsistent persona and refusal behavior
May simulate multiple charactersMaintains a single "assistant" voice
Does not acknowledge uncertaintyTrained to say "I'm not sure" where appropriate
Why SFT Is Efficient SFT doesn't need to re-teach facts — all world knowledge is already in the weights from pre-training. SFT only teaches format and disposition, which is why SFT datasets can be small relative to pre-training data.
Chapter 7

Hallucinations and the Limits of Knowledge

Why models sometimes confabulate — and how to account for it

Hallucination: the generation of confident-sounding text that is factually false. The model may invent citations, attribute quotes to people who never said them, or describe events that never occurred.

Why Hallucination Occurs

The model was trained to produce plausible continuations of text — not to verify claims. When it lacks reliable information, it does not "know that it doesn't know." Instead, it interpolates from related patterns and generates something that sounds right.

Analogy This resembles how human memory works under pressure. Asked to recall a barely-remembered fact, you may unconsciously construct a plausible answer that feels like a memory but is partly fabricated. The LLM does this constantly, at scale, without any sense that it is doing so.

Swiss Cheese Capabilities

Karpathy introduces a useful metaphor: the model's capabilities are like a block of Swiss cheese. The cheese represents vast, mostly solid reliable performance. The holes — unpredictable gaps — represent areas where the model fails at apparently simple tasks.

A model that can write elegant poetry may fail to compare 9.11 vs. 9.9. A model that can explain quantum mechanics may fail to count letters in a word. The holes are an artifact of how tokenization and fixed-width computation interact with the training data.

Practical Rules of Thumb (1) Never trust LLM citations without verifying independently. (2) Use a code interpreter for arithmetic. (3) For recent facts or statistics, verify with search. (4) Treat model output as a high-quality first draft, not a final authority.
Knowledge Cutoff Beyond hallucination, models have a cutoff: they cannot know events after their training data was collected. Questions about recent events will get stale or hallucinated answers.
Chapter 8

Context Windows and Memory

The two-memory architecture of language models

Language models have two fundamentally different kinds of memory, operating at different timescales with different properties.

Memory TypeLocationProperties
Long-termModel weightsEverything from training. Fuzzy, probabilistic, fixed after training.
WorkingContext windowThe live conversation. Precise and verbatim — but limited and ephemeral.

The context windowThe maximum number of tokens an LLM can "see" at once when generating. Modern models support 128K tokens or more. Everything outside the window is invisible. is the buffer of tokens currently being processed — the entire conversation history, injected documents, and system instructions. If the conversation exceeds the context length, older content is silently dropped.

Practical Implication Information you paste into the conversation is more reliably accessible than information from training — context is exact while trained knowledge is approximate. When accuracy matters, provide the source material directly.

RAG: Bridging the Two Memories

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)A technique where relevant documents are retrieved from a database and injected into the context window before the model generates a response, combining retrieval precision with generative fluency. finds relevant documents and injects them into the context window — combining retrieval precision with generative fluency.

Context Length Progress Early GPT models: ~2,000 tokens. GPT-4 Turbo: 128,000. Gemini 1.5 Pro: 1 million. Rapid expansion of context windows is one of the defining trends in LLM development.
Chapter 9

Tool Use and Augmented Reasoning

Extending LLMs by connecting them to external systems

A bare language model is limited by what it can compute while generating a response. But modern LLM systems can also invoke external tools — web search, code interpreters, calculators, APIs — and incorporate results into the context window.

Tool use: the model generates a structured tool call, the tool executes, its output is appended to the context, and the model continues generating based on the enriched context.

What Tools Address

  1. Web Search — Overcomes knowledge cutoff; provides current information; reduces reliance on hallucinated facts.
  2. Code Interpreter — Offloads precise computation to an exact executor that cannot hallucinate a mathematical result.
  3. APIs — Connects the model to live databases, calendars, email, and any digital service.
Strategic Principle Skilled LLM use means knowing which tasks to entrust to the model's native capabilities and which to route through tools. Mathematical precision, current information, and character-level operations should almost always go through tools.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Instructing the model to think step by step before answering significantly improves accuracy on multi-step reasoning tasks. It forces the model to spread hard computation across many tokens rather than trying to compute the answer in a single forward pass — giving it, in effect, more mental scratch paper.

Why Tools Help The model processes a fixed number of computational layers per token. Some problems require more computation than a single forward pass provides. Tools and chain-of-thought compensate for this architectural constraint.
Chapter 10

Thinking Models and Reinforcement Learning

A new class of models that reason before responding

A distinct and rapidly advancing class of models — OpenAI's o1/o3 series, DeepSeek-R1 — employs a different paradigm: rather than generating a response directly, they first produce an extended internal chain of thought, reasoning through the problem before committing to an answer.

These models are trained via reinforcement learning (RL) on tasks with verifiable correct answers — mathematics, formal logic, competitive programming — where correct answers can be automatically checked, making the training signal precise and scalable.

"In principle, this paradigm is capable of doing something really cool, new, and exciting — something even that no human has thought of before."

— Andrej Karpathy

The AlphaGo Analogy

Karpathy draws an analogy to AlphaGo's "Move 37" — a move so unexpected that human commentators initially assumed it was an error, before recognizing it as a profound strategic insight. The same dynamic may apply to reasoning models: RL training can discover reasoning strategies that no human data labeler would have written, because the model is optimizing against a ground-truth signal rather than imitating human behavior.

Current Limitations Thinking models currently excel in verifiable domains — math, code, logic. Whether learned reasoning strategies transfer to open-ended, unverifiable domains remains an open research question.
Try It DeepSeek-R1 (open weights, MIT license) is available at together.ai. OpenAI's o3-mini via ChatGPT. Both expose reasoning traces illustrating chain-of-thought in action.
Open Weights DeepSeek-R1's MIT license means anyone can download, modify, and deploy it — unprecedented for a model of its capability level.
Chapter 11

RLHF and Model Alignment

Training models to be helpful, harmless, and honest

The third training stage — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) — differs from SFT crucially: rather than showing the model what to write, it shows the model what humans prefer.

How RLHF Works

  1. Generate — The current model produces multiple candidate responses to a prompt.
  2. Compare — Human evaluators rank these responses by quality.
  3. Train a Reward Model — A separate network learns to predict human preference scores at scale.
  4. Optimize — The main model is updated via RL to produce outputs receiving high reward scores.
Why RLHF Is Powerful RLHF doesn't require humans to write ideal responses — only to compare existing ones. This is far easier and scales: the reward model can evaluate millions of responses that no human will ever read, allowing RL training to run far longer than direct labeling would permit.

Limitations and Open Questions

Models can learn to "game" the reward model — producing responses that score highly without being genuinely helpful. The reward model is an approximation of human preferences, not a ground truth, and can be miscalibrated in subtle ways. These are active areas of alignment research.

RLHF vs. RL Standard RLHF (as in GPT-4) is "closer to fine-tuning than true RL." The large-scale RL in thinking models (§10) is a more aggressive and transformative application of reinforcement learning principles.
Chapter 12

Practical Guidance: Using LLMs Effectively

A synthesis of the mental models developed throughout this text

Having built up an understanding of how LLMs work from first principles, we can now derive principled practical guidance for working with them.

The Right Mental Model

An LLM assistant is a neural network simulation of a human expert data labeler, constrained to a fixed amount of computation per token. This single framing explains most of its behavior — its fluency, its breadth, its hallucinations, and its surprising gaps.

Core Principles

  1. Use them constantly. Productivity gains from heavy LLM use are large and compounding. Calibrate intuitions through direct, frequent experience.
  2. Own the output. Treat all LLM-generated work as a draft you review and take responsibility for.
  3. Verify important claims. For any factual assertion that matters — citations, statistics, recent events — verify independently.
  4. Route to tools when precision matters. Arithmetic, string manipulation, current events: use tools. The model's native capabilities are statistical and approximate; tools are exact.
  5. Give it room to think. Ask the model to reason step by step before answering complex problems. This significantly improves reliability.
  6. Provide relevant context. Paste in relevant source material rather than relying on trained recall. Working memory is more reliable than long-term memory.

Key Takeaways
  • LLMs are built in three stages: pre-training (knowledge), SFT (helpfulness), RLHF (alignment).
  • A model response is a neural simulation of a human expert — not a database lookup.
  • Hallucinations are an inherent consequence of probabilistic text prediction.
  • Context windows are more reliable than trained knowledge; provide source material when accuracy matters.
  • Use tools (search, code) for tasks requiring precision or currency.
  • Thinking models (o1, DeepSeek-R1) use RL to discover reasoning strategies beyond human imitation.
  • Use LLMs frequently, critically, and with verification — as tools in a toolbox, not oracles.
